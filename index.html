The Great Barrier Reef in Crisis

How 400-year ocean heat records and human-induced climate change are threatening one of the world's most vital natural wonders.

The Great Barrier Reef spans over 2,300 kilometers off Queensland, Australia, supporting thousands of marine species.

A Global Ecosystem Under Threat

The Great Barrier Reef is the largest coral reef system on Earth. However, human-induced climate change is putting this unique ecosystem at immediate risk. According to the Queensland Government State of the Environment report, climate change is the single most pervasive and persistent influence on the long-term outlook of the Great Barrier Reef.

The most urgent threats are rising ocean temperatures that push corals beyond their thermal limits, triggering mass coral bleaching and mortality. These heatwaves also combine with agricultural runoff, ocean acidification, and intense cyclones, shrinking the recovery window the reef needs to survive.

6 Critical Facts About Climate Threats to the Reef

The following six facts are drawn directly from peer-reviewed research published in Nature (reported by Earth.org) and the Queensland Government State of the Environment report:

1. Highest Ocean Warming in 400 Years

A study published in the journal Nature found that Coral Sea temperatures between January and March in 2017, 2020, and 2024 were the hottest in 400 years. Scientists reconstructed four centuries of sea surface temperatures using drill cores taken from long-lived coral skeletons.

Source: Earth.org / Nature (August 2024)

2. Directly Driven by Human Emissions

Climate attribution modeling confirms that recent extreme ocean warming is indisputably caused by human greenhouse gas emissions from burning fossil fuels. Natural climate cycles like El Niño alone cannot explain the unprecedented heat recorded since 1900.

Source: Earth.org / Climate Attribution Research

3. Bleaching Has Become Near-Annual

Mass coral bleaching was historically a rare event happening once every few decades. The Great Barrier Reef has now suffered 5 mass bleaching events in just 8 years, giving corals virtually no recovery window to regrow their essential algae.

Source: Earth.org Environmental Analysis

4. Climate Change Is Ranked Threat #1

The Queensland State of the Environment report identifies climate change as the single greatest, most persistent, and most damaging threat to the Great Barrier Reef, far exceeding localized pressures like coastal development or direct use.

Source: Queensland State of the Environment

5. Extreme Heat Exceeds Corals' Thermal Limits

When ocean temperatures stay above average for prolonged periods, corals experience extreme thermal stress and expel the microscopic algae (zooxanthellae) living in their tissues. Without this algae, corals turn white, starve, and can suffer widespread mortality.

Source: Queensland Government DETSI Indicators

6. Compounding Pressures Weaken Resilience

Marine heatwaves interact with other major environmental threats, including ocean acidification, severe tropical cyclones, and sediment and pesticide runoff from land catchments. These compounding pressures severely weaken the reef’s natural ability to recover.

Source: Queensland Reef 2050 Long-Term Plan

Rising Sea Temperatures and Coral Bleaching

As global greenhouse emissions rise, the world's oceans absorb more than 90% of the excess heat. Corals are highly sensitive to even small temperature increases. A rise of just 1°C to 1.5°C above the typical summer maximum can cause widespread coral bleaching across hundreds of reefs.

In 2016 and 2017, the Great Barrier Reef experienced back-to-back summers of severe mass coral bleaching, which caused unprecedented coral loss in the northern sections. While fast-growing corals can partially recover if water temperatures return to normal, repeated heatwaves destroy mature coral colonies that take decades to grow.

Bleached corals lose their colorful symbiotic algae when water temperatures exceed safe thermal limits. (Photo: Marine Science Archive)

The 1.5°C vs 2.0°C Global Survival Threshold

According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), limiting global warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels is critical for coral reefs. At 1.5°C of warming, between 10% and 30% of global coral reefs are projected to survive. If warming reaches 2.0°C, scientists estimate that over 99% of all coral reefs worldwide will be permanently lost.

Reference: Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC Report)
